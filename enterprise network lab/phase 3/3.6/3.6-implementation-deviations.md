# 3.6 Implementation Decisions and Deviations

## Purpose

Sub-stages 3.1 to 3.5 specified what the network requires and how it is built.
This document records what happened when that specification met the
implementation environment: the decisions taken during the build, and every point
where the design could not be implemented as written.

The handling principle is the one established in 3.3.10 and applied throughout:
**the design is not rewritten to match the tool**. Where a capability is
unavailable, the requirement stands and the shortfall is recorded with its
consequence. A gap documented with its consequence is a known limitation; a gap
left unrecorded is a defect nobody will notice until it matters.

---

## A Note on the Implementation Environment

Cisco Packet Tracer does not execute the operating systems of the devices it
models. Each device implements a subset of the corresponding CLI, selected for
teaching purposes. The deviations below follow from that constraint rather than
from any weakness in the design: on physical equipment, every requirement
recorded here as unimplementable is fully supported.

The constraint was anticipated. Sub-stage 3.3.1 states it explicitly, noting that
device capabilities in a simulation environment differ from the physical products
they represent, and that anything which could not be confirmed from the catalogue
was marked **to verify** rather than assumed either way. Several of the items
below are those verifications resolving in the negative.

Deviations are classified as:

| Type | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Implementation decision** | The design left the choice open; it was settled during the build |
| **Platform limitation** | The design specified something the environment cannot do |
| **Design correction** | The build revealed an error or omission in the design documents |

## 1. Deviation Register

### D1 — Point-to-point addressing on the firewall-to-edge link

**Type:** Design correction

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.4.4 and the routing tables in 2.5.4 assign `10.0.254.5`
to the firewall and `10.0.254.6` to the edge router.

**What happened.** Sub-stages 3.5.6 and 3.5.7 reverse these two addresses. The
contradiction was identified before configuration began.

**Resolution.** The Phase 2 assignment was retained, because the routing tables in
2.5.4 depend on it: the firewall takes `.5` and the edge router `.6`. Sub-stages
3.5.6 and 3.5.7 have been corrected to match.

The link itself subsequently moved out of `10.0.254.0/24` altogether — see D2 —
so the final addresses are `203.0.113.5` and `203.0.113.6`. The two errors were
independent: this one put the right pair of host numbers on the wrong ends, and
D2 put the pair in the wrong address space. Of the two, only D2 broke anything.

---

### D2 — Addressing of the outside link

**Type:** Design correction

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.4.4 places the firewall-to-edge link inside
`10.0.254.0/24`, the range reserved for internal point-to-point links. Sub-stage
2.5.6 separately states that PAT uses "a single public address from the ISP".

**What happened.** The two statements are incompatible. The firewall-to-edge link
is where PAT terminates, so its address is what the outside world sees. With an
RFC 1918 address there, translated traffic left the network with a source the ISP
cannot route. Diagnosed with `debug ip icmp` on the ISP router, which reported
`host unreachable` for every returning packet.

**Resolution.** The link was moved to `203.0.113.4/30`, with the firewall at `.5`
and the edge router at `.6`. Static routes on the firewall, edge router and ISP
router were updated accordingly.

**Consequence.** None adverse. The change aligns the implementation with what
2.5.6 already assumed. It corrects an omission rather than introducing a
compromise: the link was treated as internal when it is in fact the boundary at
which the internal network becomes externally visible. Sub-stage 2.4.4 has been
restructured to distinguish internal point-to-point links from boundary links,
with the principle stated generally: **a link is internal or external according to
what is on the far side of it, not according to which rack it sits in.**

---

### D3 — Guest VLAN handoff mode

**Type:** Implementation decision, constrained by platform

**Design.** Sub-stage 3.4.4 left the Core→ASA Guest handoff explicitly **TBD**,
offering two options: an untagged access port, or a tagged trunk with a
subinterface on the firewall.

**What happened.** The tagged option was attempted first. The firewall rejected
`interface GigabitEthernet1/3.60`: subinterfaces are not implemented on this
platform.

**Resolution.** Untagged access port. The core switch places `Gi1/0/4` in VLAN 60
as an access port; the firewall receives the traffic on `Gi1/3` with
`10.0.60.1/24`.

**Consequence.** None. Only one VLAN crosses the link, so tagging would convey no
information, and the native-VLAN consideration does not arise. The design
anticipated both options and the platform selected one of them.

---

### D4 — Corporate NAT object definition

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.5.6 requires separate translation contexts for corporate
and Guest traffic, so that Guest activity remains identifiable in translation and
firewall logs (F6).

**What happened.** The corporate object was initially defined as `10.0.0.0/16`,
which covers every internal VLAN including Guest. On physical equipment this
would not matter: traffic arriving on the `guest` interface cannot match a rule
written `(inside,outside)`. The simulated firewall does not evaluate the source
interface, so all Guest traffic matched the corporate rule. Confirmed with
`show nat`: 35 hits on the corporate rule, zero on the Guest rule.

**Resolution.** The single object was replaced with four, one per corporate VLAN
(`10.0.10.0/24`, `10.0.20.0/24`, `10.0.30.0/24`, `10.0.40.0/24`). Separation
verified: both rules subsequently accumulated hits independently.

**Consequence.** Positive beyond the immediate fix. The narrower definition means
the server segment and the management VLAN no longer have a translated path
outward, which the zone interaction matrix in 2.6.3 never granted them. The
original `/16` was broader than the policy it served.

---

### D5 — Guest DHCP

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.4.3 places the Guest gateway on the firewall, and 2.4.7
defines a DHCP scope for VLAN 60 served from there (F12): pool `.100–.199`,
public resolver, two-hour lease.

**What happened.** The firewall accepts and stores `dhcpd address
10.0.60.100-10.0.60.199 guest`, and `show dhcpd state` reports the interface as
configured for DHCP service. The command `dhcpd enable guest` is nonetheless
rejected with *"need to define address pool range first"* — the device
contradicting its own configuration. Attempted with alternative syntax, a
narrower pool, repetition within one session, and with the global lease setting
removed. Confirmed against a connected client: `DHCP request failed`, while
static addressing on the same client reaches the gateway and the Internet
normally.

**Resolution.** Not implemented. Guest clients in the lab are given static
addresses from the `.100–.199` range.

**Consequence.** Visitors cannot obtain addresses automatically, which in a real
deployment would make the Guest network unusable. The design is unchanged and
would function on physical equipment. Notably, the two-hour lease (`dhcpd lease
7200`) *is* accepted by the firewall and remains in the configuration; it simply
never takes effect, because the service never starts.

---

### D6 — DHCP lease duration on the core switch

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.4.7 specifies an eight-hour lease for the four corporate
scopes.

**What happened.** The core switch does not implement the `lease` command within
a DHCP pool.

**Resolution.** The scopes operate with the platform default.

**Consequence.** Minor. Lease duration affects address reclamation under
pressure, and the four scopes have 100 addresses each for a maximum of 42 users.

---

### D7 — External DNS forwarding

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Requirements S1b and S1c specify that the internal DNS server
forwards queries it is not authoritative for to an approved upstream resolver,
with the forwarder list constrained so that firewall rule 1 can restrict the
destination.

**What happened.** The DNS service provides no forwarder or recursion setting.
The record-type selector offers only A, AAAA, CNAME, NS and SOA; an NS record
does not produce forwarding, because the service performs no recursive lookup.

**Resolution.** Not implemented. Internal name resolution works as designed;
external names do not resolve. Internet connectivity is demonstrated by IP
address.

**Consequence.** Firewall rule 1, which permits the DNS server to reach approved
resolvers on port 53, is configured but never matched by live traffic. The
exfiltration control it implements — constraining the destination rather than
only the protocol — cannot be demonstrated. Sub-stage 3.3.8 identified the
simulated server as the least certain element of the design; this is one of the
ways that uncertainty resolved.

### D8 — Native VLAN on wireless-facing trunks

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.3.7 sets the native VLAN to 999 on all trunks, so that
untagged frames arrive in a VLAN with no SVI anywhere and double-tagging has no
destination to reach.

**What happened.** The wireless controller sends its management traffic untagged
and offers no VLAN identifier field on its management interface. With native VLAN
999 on the core trunk, the controller was unreachable (`ping` 0/5). The setup
wizard does expose a Management VLAN ID field, which was set to 99; this had no
effect on the controller's behaviour, confirmed by restoring native 999 and
observing the loss of reachability again. The same applies to the two access
points: with VLAN 99 tagged on their switch ports, both dropped to `DOWN` and
lost their addresses.

**Resolution.** Native VLAN 99 on three links only: the core-to-controller trunk
and the two access-point trunks. All other trunks retain native VLAN 999.

**Consequence.** The VLAN-hopping mitigation is absent on three links. The
residual risk is low and bounded: each of the three terminates on a controlled
infrastructure device rather than a user-accessible port, and double-tagging
requires an attacker positioned on the native VLAN of a trunk they can inject
into. Untagged management on the native VLAN is also the conventional arrangement
for controller deployments. Sub-stage 2.3.7 now records this exception in a table
of its own rather than claiming a blanket rule, and 3.5.8 lists the three links.

---

### D9 — Guest captive portal

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.8.6 and requirement W5 specify an open Guest SSID with a
captive portal requiring acceptance of terms of use, serving two purposes: a
legal record of acceptance, and a basic access control point.

**What happened.** The controller exposes the Web Policy setting under Layer 3
security but states explicitly that the feature is not supported.

**Resolution.** Not implemented. The Guest SSID operates as an open network with
no portal.

**Consequence.** The terms-of-use record, which is the primary purpose of the
portal, does not exist. Guest access itself is unaffected: isolation is enforced
by the firewall rule and by the absence of a core SVI, not by the portal.
Sub-stage 3.3.7 flagged this as likely to be unavailable.

---

### D10 — Access point operating mode

**Type:** Implementation decision, constrained by platform

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.8.5 describes autonomous access points that tag each
frame according to the SSID its client joined and deliver it to the switch over
an 802.1Q trunk. Sub-stage 3.3.7 already records the substitution of a controller
and two lightweight access points, because the simulated autonomous model exposes
only one SSID.

**What happened.** The lightweight access points operate in FlexConnect mode and
cannot be changed to Local mode; the configuration page reports the feature as
unsupported. In FlexConnect, an access point switches client traffic locally at
the switch rather than tunnelling it to the controller. The WLANs had been mapped
to dynamic interfaces on the controller, which assumes Local mode. The result was
that no SSID was broadcast at all: clients detected no wireless networks, not
even the open Guest SSID.

**Resolution.** Two changes. The access-point switch ports were converted from
access ports to 802.1Q trunks carrying VLANs 10, 20, 30, 40, 60 and 99. Each WLAN
was configured with **FlexConnect Local Switching** and **FlexConnect Local
Auth**.

**Consequence.** None adverse, and the result is closer to the design than the
controller architecture suggests. The access point now delivers tagged client
traffic directly to the switch, which is precisely what 2.8.5 specifies for the
autonomous design. Verified with a wireless client: each corporate SSID placed it
in its department's VLAN, with the correct gateway and DNS server obtained by
DHCP.

### D11 — VPN termination point

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Design decision DD-1 and sub-stage 2.9.2 terminate remote-access VPN
on the firewall, so that remote traffic is subject to the same enforcement point
as all other traffic by construction. Sub-stage 2.9.2 names the failure this
avoids: terminating behind the enforcement point would let remote users bypass
the policy that applies to everyone else.

**What happened.** The firewall accepts `tunnel-group <name> type remote-access`
but implements no mechanism for assigning addresses to remote clients. There is
no `ip local pool` at global configuration level, no `address-pool` within
tunnel-group general attributes — which offer only `default-group-policy` — and
nothing within group-policy attributes, which offer only `vpn-tunnel-protocol`
and `webvpn`. The WebVPN subcommand set contains only `url-list`, which presents
a page of links and establishes no tunnel. Without address assignment there is no
remote-access VPN.

**Resolution.** The tunnel terminates on the edge router using IPsec with group
authentication and local user accounts. Two address pools are defined there,
`10.0.201.0/24` for Finance and `10.0.202.0/24` for IT, preserving the
requirement of 2.9.3 that the address is the identity.

**Why this arrangement and not the obvious alternative.** Terminating on the edge
router with direct access to the internal network was considered and rejected: it
would place remote users inside the perimeter without passing the enforcement
point, which is exactly what 2.9.2 forbids. The arrangement adopted avoids this.
The edge router has no route to the internal VLANs except through the firewall,
so every packet from a VPN client toward an internal destination transits the
firewall and is evaluated against rules 7 and 8.

**Consequence.** What moves is encryption and address assignment. What does not
move is policy enforcement. Verified: a remote Finance user reaches the DNS
server but is denied the management VLAN; the same workstation, connecting with
IT credentials, receives an address from the other pool and reaches the
management VLAN. Requirement F9 — which 3.1.3 identifies as the requirement whose
failure carries the greatest security consequence — is satisfied.

**Residual difference.** Encryption now occurs at a device outside the firewall.
In a real deployment this would warrant review, because it means the tunnel
endpoint is not itself protected by the firewall.

### D12 — NAT exemption for VPN traffic

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.5.6 applies PAT to Internet-bound traffic. Traffic between
VPN clients and internal networks is not Internet-bound and is not translated —
the standard arrangement on any firewall.

**What happened.** The firewall applied PAT to replies from corporate VLANs
toward the VPN pools, rewriting the source address so that the remote client
received answers from an address it had not queried and discarded them. Confirmed
with `show xlate`: `ICMP PAT from inside:10.0.20.100 to outside:203.0.113.5`. No
exemption is possible: manual NAT does not exist on this platform, and the `nat`
command within a network object accepts only `dynamic` or `static` with no
destination qualifier.

**Resolution.** See D13.

---

### D13 — Location of PAT

**Type:** Platform limitation, consequence of D12

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.5.6 places translation on the firewall, so that policy and
translation are applied at the same boundary and appear together in the same
logs.

**What happened.** With no exemption available (D12), the only way to stop the
firewall translating VPN-bound replies was to stop it translating at all.

**Resolution.** PAT moved to the edge router. Two access lists preserve the
separation that 2.5.6 requires: list 150 for the four corporate VLANs, list 151
for Guest. Each denies translation toward the two VPN pools — a deny in a NAT
access list exempts rather than drops — and permits it toward everything else.
The firewall retains all access control and no longer performs translation.

**Verification.** Internal hosts reach the Internet with translation visible on
the edge router (`10.0.20.100 → 203.0.113.2`) and no translations on the firewall
(`show xlate`: 0 in use). VPN clients reach internal workstations with the real
source address preserved.

**Consequence.** Policy and translation are now recorded on different devices.
Sub-stage 2.5.6 chose the firewall specifically so that an investigator could see,
in one place, both who attempted what and how it was translated. That correlation
is lost. The separation of corporate and Guest translation contexts (F6) is
preserved, by means of the two separate access lists.

### D14 — Stateful inspection and the outside access list

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.7.4 states that the firewall performs stateful inspection:
when a permitted connection is established, the response is recognised as
belonging to a known session and allowed automatically, so rules are written only
for the direction that initiates a connection. Requirement F1 identifies this as
the most important property of the device, and 3.1.3 warns that writing rules in
both directions would grant exactly the initiation right the design intends to
withhold.

**What happened.** After an inbound access list was applied to the outside
interface, replies to connections initiated from inside were discarded. On
physical equipment these replies are matched against the connection table before
the access list is consulted and never reach it.

**Resolution.** An explicit rule permitting ICMP echo-reply was added to the
outside access list.

**Consequence.** One rule exists that would be unnecessary, and arguably
inadvisable, on physical equipment. It is narrow — echo-reply only — and does not
grant inbound initiation rights for any other traffic. The requirement stands as
written; the rule is an artefact of the environment.

### D15 — SNMP

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.10.5 specifies SNMPv3 agents on every infrastructure
device, read-only, with v1 and v2c rejected because the community string travels
in clear text. Sub-stage 2.10.4 specifies both polling and traps, explaining that
each covers what the other misses: polling establishes a baseline but has
detection latency, traps report immediately but say nothing about a device that
has failed completely. Requirements C10, A8, E5, F14, S3b, S3c and S9 all depend
on this.

**What happened.** Three findings, each independently disqualifying. The devices
accept only `snmp-server community`, with no `snmp-server group` or
`snmp-server user` for v3. There is no `snmp-server host` and no `enable traps`,
so no device can send a notification. And the server provides no SNMP service at
all, so there is nothing to receive either polls or traps.

**Resolution.** SNMP is not enabled anywhere.

**Why nothing was configured.** With no collector, an enabled agent would serve
no purpose while presenting a clear-text community string to anyone observing the
network. Sub-stage 2.10.3 states the principle directly: unencrypted management
protocols are a defect, and the availability of a service on a device is itself a
risk even when it is not used. The same reasoning that disabled Telnet and HTTP
applies here. Configuring an agent to appear complete, while leaving it
unreachable and insecure, would be worse than not configuring it.

**Consequence.** Performance monitoring — CPU, memory, interface utilisation — is
not available, and no device reports events as they occur. Monitoring rests on
Syslog and ICMP reachability alone. Sub-stage 3.3.8 identified the monitoring
server as the uncertain element of the design and 3.3.10 recorded that, if the
services proved unavailable, the monitoring design would be specified but not
demonstrated. That is what has happened.

### D16 — Per-rule logging

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.10.6 requires the firewall to forward Syslog to
`10.0.50.30` at severity level 6, and notes that this device carries particular
weight because it generates the security-relevant events the monitoring design
exists to capture: denied traffic, VPN sessions, policy matches. Sub-stage 2.7.6
and requirement F4 specify per-rule logging, with all deny rules and privileged
access logged while routine user traffic is not, so that volume does not obscure
meaningful entries.

**What happened — two findings, the second discovered later than the first.**

*The firewall does not recognise `logging` in any form.* The complete global
configuration command set contains no logging, syslog or event command. The `log`
keyword is also rejected at the end of an access list entry.

*The core switch rejects the `log` keyword as well.* This was established by test
during the documentation review, after the build was complete: entering
`40 deny ip any 10.0.99.0 0.0.0.255 log` within a named extended access list
returns `% Invalid input detected`, with the marker under `log`. The same entry
without the keyword is accepted. Sequence-numbered editing works normally, so the
rejection is of the keyword and not of the syntax around it.

**Resolution.** Not implemented on any device. Syslog operates on the switches and
the routers, carrying device events only.

**Consequence.** The most significant deviation in this document, and larger than
it first appeared. The distinction that matters is between two kinds of event:

| | Collected | Examples |
|---|:---:|---|
| **Device events** | ✅ | Interface state changes, logins, configuration changes |
| **Policy events** | ❌ | Denied traffic, management-plane access, VPN sessions, server administration |

Every category in the logging policy of 2.7.6 except the last is a policy event,
and **none of them is recorded anywhere in the build**. A visitor attempting to
reach the corporate network, a remote user connecting, a denied management
attempt — the network stops all three and remembers none of them.

The logging policy itself — which distinguishes carefully between what is worth
recording and what is noise — cannot be exercised at all. The criterion it applies,
*record what would be needed after an incident*, remains the right criterion; the
implementation produces none of it.

**Why the second finding matters beyond its content.** Until it was tested, the
expectation was that the core supported the keyword and that its absence was
implementation work still to do. Had the documentation been written on that
assumption, it would have recorded as a shortfall of the build something that
belongs to the platform — attributing to the engineer a limitation of the tool.
The test cost one command and reversed the conclusion.

### D17 — SSH administration of servers

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Requirement S7 specifies an SSH server on each host for
administration, and core ACL rule 5 permits IT workstations to reach VLAN 50 on
port 22. Sub-stage 3.1.6 distinguishes this from rules 3 and 4: those permit
*use* of a service, rule 5 permits *administration* of the host.

**What happened.** The simulated server provides no SSH service. A connection
attempt from an IT workstation returns `Connection refused` — the access list
permits the traffic and the host declines it.

**Resolution.** Not implemented. Rule 5 remains in the access list and would
function with a real host.

**Consequence.** Server administration cannot be demonstrated. The access control
is correct and verifiable by the nature of the refusal: a denial by the access
list would time out rather than be refused.

### D18 — Edge router management address

**Type:** Design correction

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.4.6 assigns `10.0.99.20` to the edge router as its
management address.

**What happened.** The edge router sits outside the firewall and has no interface
in VLAN 99. The address cannot be configured on it without a physical connection
to the management segment — which would create a path from an Internet-facing
device into the management plane, bypassing the firewall entirely and violating
requirement E2, which states that no route may bypass the enforcement point.

**Resolution.** The edge router is managed over SSH at `203.0.113.6`, its
firewall-facing interface. The address `10.0.99.20` is not used.

**Consequence.** Management traffic to this device does not traverse the
management VLAN. Access is still restricted by SSH authentication, and the device
is reachable only from the IT VLAN by way of the firewall. Sub-stage 2.4.6 has
been amended to withdraw the assignment and record how the device is actually
reached, and 2.10.3 now lists the edge router as the single exception to the
management-VLAN model.

### D19 — Time source

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.6.5 and requirement E3 make the edge router both an NTP
client, synchronising from an external source, and an NTP server distributing
time internally. The purpose, set out in 2.10.7, is log correlation: devices
taking time from a single internal source share whatever offset that source
carries, so their relative intervals remain correct.

**What happened.** The environment provides no external time source. The edge
router's clock is set manually and it serves as a local reference.

**Resolution.** The edge router operates as a stratum 3 local reference; all other
devices synchronise from it. Verified: switches report stratum 4 with the edge
router as reference, and Syslog messages carry consistent timestamps.

**Consequence.** Absolute time is correct only until the simulation is reopened.
The property that matters is unaffected: all devices agree with one another, so
events can be ordered. Sub-stage 2.10.7 makes exactly this distinction — that
consistency rather than absolute accuracy is what log correlation requires.

**A second consequence, found by failure testing.** Losing the edge router moves
the core to `unsynchronized, stratum 16` and leaves every device free-running. One
device therefore carries both the Internet path and the time reference, and its
failure produces two unrelated problems — the second of which removes the ability
to order the logs generated during the first. This strengthens the case for an
external time source beyond accuracy: it would decouple time distribution from the
Internet path.

### D20 — VPN authentication mechanism

**Type:** Platform limitation

**Design.** Sub-stage 2.9.5 requires multi-factor authentication for remote
access, on the grounds that the VPN is the only service permitting a connection to
be initiated from the Internet into the internal network. Acknowledging that MFA
needs infrastructure outside the project's scope, the sub-stage recorded the
intended implementation as **certificate-based authentication combined with user
credentials** — a per-user certificate as *something held*, a password as
*something known*. Requirement F10 names certificate authentication specifically.

**What happened.** The environment provides no public key infrastructure. There is
no certificate authority to issue or revoke per-user certificates, so the
mechanism 2.9.5 described could not be built even though it was already the
fallback from MFA.

**Resolution.** IPsec with **a shared group key per department** combined with
per-user credentials, held locally on the edge router:

| Group | Key | Users |
|-------|-----|-------|
| `VPN-FINANCE` | one shared key | per-user accounts, locally authenticated |
| `VPN-IT` | one shared key | per-user accounts, locally authenticated |

**Consequence.** The second factor is not what the design specified, and the
difference is substantive rather than terminological.

A per-user certificate is *something held*: issued to one person, installed on one
machine, so an attacker with a stolen password must also compromise that machine.
**A group key is something shared.** Every remote Finance user presents the same
key, so possession of it proves membership of a group rather than identity of a
person, and a leak requires changing it for everyone in that department.

The two factors are therefore a shared secret and a password, which is closer to
one and a half factors than to two. What remains true is that a stolen password
alone is insufficient — the attacker also needs the key. That is a real improvement
on credentials alone and is the honest limit of the claim.

**This is the same weakness as WPA2-PSK**, which 2.8.3 analyses at length for
wireless: a shared secret that authenticates a group rather than a person, with no
individual accountability. It is not a coincidence. Both rest on a shared secret
for the same reason — no authentication infrastructure exists — and the single
addition of a RADIUS server with a directory would resolve the wireless
limitation, the MFA limitation and this one together.

**Document correction.** Sub-stage 2.9.5 recorded certificate-based authentication
as the current implementation. That was never built, and the sub-stage has been
amended. This is the one deviation in this register that corrects a claim rather
than a specification.

## 2. Additions to the Firewall and ACL Design

The access control lists in 2.7.2 and 2.7.3 specify the policy the network
enforces. Implementing them revealed traffic the tables do not account for.
Without these additions the network does not function; they are recorded here so
that the design documents can be completed.

| Addition | Why required | Where |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| `permit udp any any eq 67` | DHCP requests carry source `0.0.0.0` and destination `255.255.255.255`, matching no rule written in terms of VLAN subnets. Without this, no workstation obtains an address | Inbound on VLANs 10, 20, 30 |
| `permit udp 10.0.50.0 eq 53 any` | The core applies stateless access lists. Rule 9 denies server-initiated traffic toward users; without an explicit allowance, DNS replies are denied with it | Inbound on VLAN 50 |
| `permit tcp 10.0.50.0 any established` | Same reasoning for TCP. The `established` keyword matches only packets belonging to a connection opened from the other direction | Inbound on VLAN 50 |
| `permit udp <devices> host 10.0.50.30 eq 514` | Rule 6 in 2.7.2 permits infrastructure devices to reach the monitoring server, but the core, the edge router and the switches send from addresses outside VLAN 99 | Outbound on VLAN 50 |
| `permit icmp 10.0.40.0 10.0.50.0` | Requirement §10 calls for ping-based availability checks. The rule tables permit no ICMP anywhere | Outbound on VLAN 50 |
| `permit icmp` and `permit udp eq 123` from `203.0.113.6` | NTP replies and diagnostic ICMP from the edge router. Rule 3 in 2.7.3 permits the edge router outbound NTP, but that traffic never transits the firewall; the replies inbound do | Inbound on outside |
| `deny ip <user VLAN> 10.0.99.0` | Rule 2 applied outbound on VLAN 99 does not filter traffic addressed to the core's own SVI, because such traffic never leaves the interface | Inbound on VLANs 10, 20, 30 |

The first of these is worth noting for a second reason: it was introduced after
the access lists had been applied and verified, and it broke DHCP for every
department. The failure was found by the post-implementation verification in
13.A, not during configuration.

## 4. Summary by Type

| Type | Count | Items |
|------|------:|-------|
| Design corrections | 3 | D1, D2, D18 |
| Implementation decisions | 2 | D3, D10 |
| Platform limitations | 15 | D4–D9, D11–D17, D19, D20 |

**The three design corrections are the ones that matter beyond this
implementation.** D1 is a transcription error between phases. D2 and D18 are
substantive: both concern devices and links at the network boundary, where the
distinction between internal and external addressing was not carried through
consistently. Both would have caused failures on physical equipment.

**The two most consequential platform limitations are D15 and D16**, which
together mean that the monitoring design of 2.10 is specified but largely
undemonstrated. D16 in particular means the network enforces a policy it cannot
observe: every control works and no device records a single policy event.

**Three limitations share one cause.** D15 (no SNMPv3), D20 (no PKI) and the MFA
shortfall behind it are all consequences of the environment providing no
authentication or security infrastructure. The same is true of the wireless
limitation analysed in 2.8.3. A single addition — a RADIUS server with a directory
— would resolve all four, which is why 2.8.3, 2.9.5 and 2.10.5 share a migration
trigger rather than each carrying its own.

**No deviation compromises the access control policy.** Every requirement in §4,
§6, §7, §9 and §10 concerning who may reach what was verified by test, with
results recorded in the test results document.

**What the deviations do compromise is visibility.** The policy is enforced
correctly and demonstrably. It is simply not recorded: D15 removes performance and
event monitoring, D16 removes policy logging entirely, and D13 separates the
remaining translation records from the policy they belong to. A reader should take
from this register that the network does what it was designed to do, and that it
would be difficult to prove after the fact.
