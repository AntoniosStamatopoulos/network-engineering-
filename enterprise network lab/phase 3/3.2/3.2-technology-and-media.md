# 3.2 Technology and Media Selection

This sub-stage determines the media and speeds that connect the devices specified
in 3.1. It bridges capability requirements and model selection: a requirement for
"at least nine ports" is not a purchasing specification until the port type and
speed are known.

Two genuine decisions are taken here — uplink speed and PoE standard. The
remaining content records specifications rather than choosing between meaningful
alternatives, for reasons given in 3.2.6.

---

## 3.2.1 Media Selection

**Selected: Cat6 copper throughout.**

| Link | Medium | Reason |
|------|--------|--------|
| Endpoint → access switch | Cat6 | Standard horizontal cabling; well within 100 m |
| Access switch → core | Cat6 | 10 Gbps supported to 55 m, adequate at single-site distances |
| Core → firewall, servers | Cat6 | Short runs within the same equipment area |
| Firewall → edge router | Cat6 | Short run |
| Edge router → ISP | Provider-determined | Not selected — see 3.2.5 |

### Why fibre was not selected

Fibre becomes necessary when distance exceeds copper's 100 metre limit, or when
required speeds exceed what copper carries over the distance in question. Neither
applies: the design is a single site (§1), and the longest run is well within
copper's range.

Selecting fibre would add cost, termination complexity, and transceivers, in
exchange for capability the distances do not require.

### A note on Cat6 at 10 Gbps

Cat6 carries 10 Gbps to approximately 55 metres rather than the full 100 metres
that Cat6a supports. This is sufficient for the access-to-core links in a
single-equipment-area design, but it is a constraint worth recording: if an access
switch were later placed further from the core than 55 metres, the 10 Gbps uplink
selected in 3.2.2 would require Cat6a on that run.

For a new installation where the cost difference is marginal, specifying Cat6a
throughout removes this constraint entirely.

## 3.2.2 Speed Selection

| Link | Speed | Oversubscription |
|------|-------|------------------|
| Endpoint → access switch | 1 Gbps | — |
| **Access switch → core** | **10 Gbps** | **2.4:1** |
| Core → firewall (inside and Guest links) | 1 Gbps each | — |
| Core → servers | 1 Gbps | — |
| Firewall → edge router | 1 Gbps | — |

### Decision — 10 Gbps access uplinks

Each access switch aggregates 24 Gigabit access ports. The uplink to the core
carries all of their traffic.

```text
1 Gbps uplink   →  24:1 oversubscription
10 Gbps uplink  →  2.4:1 oversubscription
```

**The 24:1 ratio was rejected.** Campus designs commonly accept ratios between
20:1 and 4:1; 24:1 sits outside that range. A design at the edge of acceptable is
not a design decision so much as an assumption that the limit will not be reached —
and an assumption of that kind is precisely what this phase exists to eliminate.

Three further considerations support the selection.

**Replacement cost.** A switch without a 10 Gbps uplink cannot be reconfigured to
provide one. When the ratio becomes constraining, the device must be **replaced**.
This is the same reasoning applied to C14 (OSPF on the core) and E6 (dynamic
routing on the edge router): a capability whose absence is paid for in hardware
belongs in the present selection even when it is not exercised immediately.

**A planned extension changes the traffic profile.** The File Server listed in §3
and §12 introduces sustained transfers toward the server segment — traffic that
does not traverse the Internet link and is therefore not limited by it. This is
exactly the profile an uplink constrains, and it is already anticipated rather
than hypothetical.

**§12 requires growth without redesign.** An uplink at its limit today is an
uplink that is inadequate with the first additional user.

### Why the Guest handoff remains 1 Gbps

VLAN 60 now reaches the firewall through a single Layer 2 handoff from the core
(2.8.4). That traffic is constrained twice before it reaches this link:

- Per-client rate limiting is applied at the access point (W7, 2.8.6)
- It terminates on the Internet connection, which for a site of this size is
  typically between 50 and 200 Mbps

There is no scenario in which a 10 Gbps Guest handoff between the core and the
firewall would relieve a constraint, because the constraint is downstream of it.

### Why core-facing links remain 1 Gbps

The core-to-firewall, core-to-server and firewall-to-edge links carry traffic that
is either limited by the Internet connection or, in the case of server access,
low-volume under the current traffic profile.

The Phase 1 traffic characterization records no sustained high-bandwidth flow:
there is no real-time media (2.10.7), no file transfer service in current scope,
and no backup or storage traffic. The highest-volume flow identified is Sales
Internet browsing, which is bursty rather than sustained and bounded by the
external link.

**The core-to-server link is the one to revisit** if the File Server is
implemented, since file transfers would make that path sustained rather than
intermittent.

## 3.2.3 Power over Ethernet

**Selected: 802.3at (PoE+), 30 W per port.**

The decision to power access points over Ethernet was taken in 3.1.2 (A11). This
determines the standard.

| Standard | Power per port | Assessment |
|----------|---------------:|------------|
| 802.3af (PoE) | 15.4 W | **Rejected** — marginal for current dual-band access points |
| **802.3at (PoE+)** | 30 W | **Selected** |
| 802.3bt (PoE++) | 60–100 W | Unnecessary; required for higher-power devices not present here |

### Why the margin matters more than the cost

The reason for selecting the higher standard is not performance but **failure
mode**.

An access point that requires PoE+ and receives only PoE does not fail cleanly. It
operates in a **degraded state**: commonly disabling one radio, reducing transmit
power, or limiting spatial streams. The device appears to function. Coverage is
worse, throughput is lower, and the cause is not apparent from the symptoms.

This would be particularly damaging in this design. Five SSIDs are already above
the usual guidance of three to four (W1, 2.8.3), and that cost was accepted on the
basis of both radios being available. If insufficient power disabled one radio,
all five SSIDs would contend for a single band and the airtime problem recorded in
W1 would worsen substantially — while the apparent cause would be a wireless
performance issue rather than a power specification.

**Diagnosing a silent degradation is considerably harder than diagnosing a clean
failure.** Selecting the higher standard removes the failure mode rather than
mitigating it.

### Power budget

A switch's PoE budget is a total across all ports rather than a per-port figure.
With **one access point per switch**, the requirement is 30 W against a budget
that is typically several hundred watts on a 24-port PoE switch.

This will not constrain selection, but the figure should be confirmed rather than
assumed — some models provide PoE on only a subset of ports.

### Scope note

PoE is a physical-layer characteristic with no representation in the
implementation environment. It is specified because 3.1 and 3.2 describe the
requirements of the **design** rather than of the simulation, and it would be a
real purchasing constraint.

## 3.2.4 Wireless Standard

**Selected: Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) minimum, dual-band.**

| Standard | Assessment |
|----------|------------|
| Wi-Fi 4 (802.11n) | Insufficient; 2.4 GHz congestion and lower throughput |
| **Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac)** | **Minimum acceptable** — widely deployed, adequate for this user count |
| Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) | Preferred if available at comparable cost; better under contention |

**Dual-band is required**, not optional. The 2.4 GHz band provides only three
non-overlapping channels and is heavily congested in most environments; 5 GHz
offers more channels and less interference. With five SSIDs broadcasting (W1),
having both bands available is what makes the airtime cost tolerable.

Wi-Fi 6 is preferred where the cost difference is small. Its advantage is less
about peak throughput than about **behaviour with many simultaneous clients**,
which is the condition that matters when a single access point serves twenty-one
users.

## 3.2.5 WAN Interface

**Not selected — determined by the service provider.**

The provider delivers a specific connection type, and the design must match it.
This was recorded in 3.1.4 (E1) as a constraint imposed from outside the design.

A mismatch cannot be resolved by configuration. The interface type should be
confirmed with the provider before the edge router is selected, since it is the
one specification in this phase that cannot be decided internally.

**Assumed for this design:** Ethernet handoff, which is the common arrangement for
business connections at this scale. The assumption is recorded because it affects
edge router selection in 3.3.

## 3.2.6 What Was Not a Decision

Most of this sub-stage records specifications rather than weighing alternatives,
and it is worth saying why rather than presenting every entry as a choice.

| Item | Why there was no real alternative |
|------|-----------------------------------|
| Copper rather than fibre | Single site; all distances well within copper's range |
| Cat6 rather than Cat5e | Cat5e cannot carry 10 Gbps at all; the uplink decision excludes it |
| 1 Gbps to endpoints | The current standard; slower would be a deliberate downgrade |
| WAN interface type | Determined externally (3.2.5) |

Recording these explicitly serves a purpose: it distinguishes decisions from
defaults. A reader can see which entries were weighed and which were determined by
circumstance, rather than having to infer it.

## 3.2.7 Effect on Device Requirements

Two requirements in 3.1 become more specific as a result of the speed and Layer 2
transit decisions:

| Requirement | Original | Revised |
|-------------|----------|---------|
| **C13** (core ports) | At least 8 ports | At least 9 ports, **of which 2 at 10 Gbps** for access uplinks |
| **A10** (access uplinks) | At least 2 uplink ports | One **10 Gbps** trunk to the core; VLAN 60 uses the same link |

Both documents have been updated. Layer 2 transit removes the dedicated Guest
uplink from each access switch and adds one core-to-firewall Guest handoff. The
core minimum therefore becomes nine ports, while each access switch needs one
10 Gbps design uplink to the core.
