# 3.2 — Purpose

What technology and media selection covers, and what it is for.

> Companion note. The selections themselves are in `3.2-technology-and-media.md`.

---

## 1. Where This Sits

Sub-stage 3.1 defined what each device must be **capable of**. Sub-stage 3.3 will
select **models**. This sub-stage bridges them.

```text
3.1:  "the core needs at least 8 ports"
        ↓
3.2:  "those ports are Gigabit Ethernet over Cat6"
        ↓
3.3:  "so the model must offer 8+ Gigabit ports"
```

Without it, model selection would silently assume speeds and media that nobody
decided. A requirement for "8 ports" is not a purchasing specification until the
port type is known.

---

## 2. The Two Questions

### Which medium

Copper or fibre, and which grade:

| Type | Speed | Distance | Typical use |
|------|-------|----------|-------------|
| Cat5e | 1 Gbps | 100 m | Older installations |
| Cat6 | 1 Gbps (10G to 55 m) | 100 m | The current default for endpoints |
| Cat6a | 10 Gbps | 100 m | Uplinks, future headroom |
| Fibre | 10+ Gbps | Kilometres | Between buildings, backbone |

The criterion is **distance and required speed**. Within a single site, copper is
almost always sufficient; fibre becomes necessary when distance exceeds copper's
100 metre limit, or when speeds beyond copper's practical range are needed.

### Which speed

Not a single answer, because access ports and uplinks are decided separately.

---

## 3. The Uplink Principle

**An uplink must be faster than the access ports it aggregates**, or it becomes a
bottleneck.

```text
24 ports × 1 Gbps on an access switch
         ↓
   1 Gbps uplink   → all 24 share 1 Gbps
   10 Gbps uplink  → comfortable
```

### Oversubscription

In practice the uplink need not match the sum, because endpoints rarely transmit
simultaneously at full rate. The **oversubscription ratio** expresses how much
capacity is shared:

```text
24 Gbps access ÷ 1 Gbps uplink   = 24:1
24 Gbps access ÷ 10 Gbps uplink  = 2.4:1
```

Campus designs commonly accept ratios between 20:1 and 4:1, depending on what the
users actually do. The ratio is a starting point for judgement rather than a
threshold: what matters is whether the traffic the network actually carries fits
through the uplink.

---

## 4. What Informs the Decision

This is where the Phase 1 traffic characterization becomes useful.

The volume estimates recorded there — very low, low, moderate, high — were
qualitative, and their purpose was not immediately obvious at the time. Here they
answer a specific question: **does the chosen speed accommodate the expected
load?**

```text
Sales → Internet:  high (20 users, browsing)
HR → DNS:          very low
```

The connection must be made explicitly. Without it, a speed selection is an
assumption rather than a decision — and an assumption cannot be defended when
questioned.

For a network of this size carrying best-effort traffic, Gigabit access is
comfortable. Stating why is what distinguishes a decision from a default.

---

## 5. What Else This Sub-Stage Covers

### Power over Ethernet

The decision to power access points over Ethernet was taken in 3.1.2 (A11). This
sub-stage determines **which standard**:

| Standard | Power per port |
|----------|---------------:|
| 802.3af (PoE) | 15.4 W |
| 802.3at (PoE+) | 30 W |
| 802.3bt (PoE++) | 60–100 W |

**The trap worth knowing:** a current-generation access point frequently requires
PoE+ rather than PoE. Given insufficient power it may operate in a **degraded
mode** — commonly disabling one radio — rather than failing outright, which makes
the fault harder to diagnose than a clean failure would be.

The switch's total **power budget** matters as well, since it is a figure across
all ports rather than per port.

### Wireless standard

Which Wi-Fi generation the access points implement:

| Standard | Characteristic |
|----------|----------------|
| Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) | Widely deployed, sufficient for most office use |
| Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) | Better with **many simultaneous clients** |

The improvement in Wi-Fi 6 is less about peak throughput than about behaviour
under contention — which matters where many devices share an access point.

### WAN interface

A special case: **this is not selected**.

The service provider delivers a specific connection type, and the selection is
constrained to matching it. This was noted in 3.1.4 (E1) as a constraint imposed
from outside the design, and it cannot be resolved by configuration if mismatched.

## 6. Who Does This Work

Worth stating plainly, because the boundaries are not obvious.

| Activity | Usually performed by |
|----------|---------------------|
| Speed and uplink selection | Network engineer |
| Standards selection (PoE, Wi-Fi generation, cable grade) | Network engineer |
| Physical cable installation | Structured cabling contractor, to a specification |
| RF design — access point placement, channels, power | Wireless specialist in larger organisations; the same engineer in smaller ones |
| WAN circuit type | Determined by the service provider |
| Electrical infrastructure | Electrician |

The engineer's role in cabling is to **write the specification** — grade, distance
limits, outlet counts — not to run the cable. Installation is then verified by
certification testing against that specification.

### How explicitly it is done in practice

**In a new installation or an upgrade:** explicitly. There is a specification,
there are decisions, and there is documentation — because someone is paying and
wants to know what they are buying.

**In day-to-day work:** far more informally. "Cat6, as everywhere." "Take the 10G
model, the other one will constrain us in two years." The knowledge is present,
but it is rarely written into a document with sections.

**Where it is always explicit:** tenders and quotations, new buildings, data
centre design, and anything requiring budget approval.

---

## 7. Why This Sub-Stage Is Short Here

This will be the briefest document in Phase 3, for reasons specific to this
project:

- **One site, short distances** — Cat6 throughout, with no real alternative to
  weigh
- **No physical cabling** — the implementation is a simulation
- **No RF design** — the implementation environment does not model it
- **The WAN type is assumed** — there is no actual provider to constrain it

Two genuine decisions remain:

1. **Uplink speed** — 1 Gbps or 10 Gbps
2. **PoE standard** — 802.3af or 802.3at

Everything else is recording a specification rather than making a choice.

### Why it is still worth writing

Two reasons.

The two decisions are real and feed directly into 3.3. An uplink speed affects
which models qualify; a PoE standard affects both the switch and the access point
selection.

And a document stating "media and speeds were considered, here is what was chosen
and why" closes the gap between 3.1 and 3.3. Without it, model selection would
rest on speeds nobody decided — which is exactly the kind of implicit assumption
this phase exists to eliminate.
