# 3.1.5 Device Requirements — Access Points

Quantity: **2** — one per access switch (2.8.5)

This document specifies what each access point must be capable of. It does not
select a model — that is 3.3. Each requirement is derived from a decision already
made in Phase 1 or Phase 2, and the derivation is shown so that no requirement
rests on assumption.

An access point is a **bridge, not a router**: it receives frames over radio,
applies a VLAN tag, and hands them to the switch (2.8.5). All routing and policy
decisions are made by the core and the firewall exactly as they are for wired
clients, which is why no wireless-specific policy exists in this design.

## Derivations

### W1 — At least 5 simultaneous SSIDs

**Source:** 2.8.2 — the SSID plan.

| SSID | VLAN |
|------|-----:|
| `CORP-HR` | 10 |
| `CORP-FINANCE` | 20 |
| `CORP-SALES` | 30 |
| `CORP-IT` | 40 |
| `GUEST` | 60 |

Five SSIDs must be broadcast concurrently by each access point.

**This is the requirement most likely to constrain selection**, though not for the
reason it first appears. Most access points support eight to sixteen SSIDs per
radio, so the technical ceiling is not the issue.

The issue is **airtime**. Each SSID transmits beacon frames roughly ten times per
second, and beacons are sent at the lowest supported data rate so that every
device can receive them. They therefore consume airtime disproportionate to their
size. Five SSIDs produce fifty beacons per second before any user data is carried.

Sub-stage 2.8.3 acknowledges this directly: *"Five SSIDs exceeds the usual
guidance... The cost is measurable but tolerable at this user count and access
point density, and it is accepted knowingly."*

**Consequence for device selection.** Verify the supported SSID count, but treat
the performance implication as the real constraint. A device advertising sixteen
SSIDs is not thereby suited to broadcasting sixteen.

### W2 — Per-SSID VLAN assignment

**Source:** 2.8.2 — each SSID maps to the VLAN its department already occupies.

This is a **separate requirement from W1**, and the distinction matters. An access
point may support five SSIDs while placing all of their traffic into a single
VLAN. What is required is that each SSID maps to a **different** VLAN, so that a
wireless HR user lands in VLAN 10 and is subject to exactly the same core ACL
rules as a wired HR user.

Sub-stage 2.8.2 states the purpose: *"no wireless-specific policy exists or is
needed."* That property depends entirely on this requirement. Without per-SSID
VLAN mapping, wireless users would occupy one segment regardless of department,
and the segmentation established in 2.3 would not extend to them.

### W3 — 802.1Q trunk uplink

**Source:** 2.8.5.

```text
| AP → Access switch | VLAN 10, 20, 30, 40, 60 | All SSIDs served by that AP |
```

The access point tags each frame with the VLAN corresponding to the SSID its
client joined, and sends all five VLANs over a single uplink. Without 802.1Q
tagging the switch could not distinguish them, and the per-SSID mapping in W2
would have no effect beyond the access point itself.

### W4 — WPA2-PSK

**Source:** 2.8.3 — static VLAN assignment with a pre-shared key per department.

The four corporate SSIDs each use WPA2 with their own passphrase. Sub-stage 2.8.3
records what this accepts: a shared passphrase per department, no individual
accountability, and no technical binding between a user and their own department —
someone who learns another department's passphrase can join that SSID and be
placed in its VLAN. Segregation depends on passphrases not circulating, which is
an administrative control rather than a technical one.

That trade-off belongs to the wireless design rather than to device selection. The
requirement here is simply that WPA2-PSK is supported, which is universal on
current equipment.

### W5 — Open SSID with captive portal

**Source:** 2.8.6.

The Guest SSID is open — no passphrase — with a captive portal presented on
association requiring acceptance of terms of use. Sub-stage 2.8.6 gives two
purposes: a legal record that the visitor accepted the terms, and a basic access
control point.

**A consequence worth noting.** Sub-stage 2.8.7 records that because the Guest
SSID is open, guest traffic is **unencrypted at the radio layer**. This is
accepted because Guest carries no corporate data and reaches only the Internet,
but it is a real limitation rather than an oversight.

**Consequence for device selection.** Captive portal functionality varies
considerably between models. Some provide it natively; others require an external
portal server or a controller. If the selected model cannot provide it, the
requirement is unmet and the legal record it exists to produce does not exist
either.

### W6 — Client isolation, configurable per SSID

**Source:** 2.8.6.

> *"Guest devices cannot communicate with one another, even within VLAN 60...
> It is not applied to corporate SSIDs, where users may have legitimate reasons to
> reach each other's devices."*

Without isolation, one visitor could scan for and attack another — a client's
laptop in reception, for example — since both occupy the same VLAN and their
traffic never leaves the access point.

**The critical word is "per SSID".** Many access points implement client isolation
as a **global** setting: enabled for all SSIDs or none. That is a genuine
constraint rather than a configuration detail, and it forces a choice:

| If only global isolation is available | Consequence |
|---------------------------------------|-------------|
| Enable it | Corporate wireless users cannot reach each other's devices — file sharing, printing between peers, and screen sharing break |
| Disable it | Guest devices can reach one another; the protection 2.8.6 specifies is absent |

Neither is satisfactory. This should be verified before selection rather than
discovered during configuration.

### W7 — Per-client bandwidth limiting

**Source:** 2.8.6 — *"A per-client rate limit prevents visitor traffic from
consuming capacity that employees depend on."*

Guest traffic is classified best-effort in the Phase 1 traffic characterization
(§3.2) and should not compete with corporate traffic on the shared Internet link.

**Per client rather than per SSID.** A limit applied to the Guest SSID as a whole
would be shared among all visitors, so a single device could consume it. A
per-client limit constrains each device independently.

### W8 — PoE powered

**Source:** decision recorded in 3.1.2 (A11).

Power over Ethernet was selected over local mains power because access points are
normally mounted where radio coverage is best — ceilings, high on walls, open
areas — and these are rarely locations with convenient mains sockets. Requiring a
socket at every access point either constrains placement to where power exists,
compromising coverage, or requires electrical work at each location.

**Consequence for device selection.** Two figures must be checked together:

- The **PoE standard** the access point requires. Current devices frequently need
  **802.3at (PoE+, 30W)** rather than 802.3af (15.4W). An access point given
  insufficient power may operate in a degraded mode — commonly disabling one radio
  — rather than failing outright, which makes the fault harder to diagnose.
- The switch's **power budget**, which is a total across all ports rather than a
  per-port figure. With one access point per switch this is unlikely to bind, but
  it should be confirmed rather than assumed.

### W9 — WPA2-Enterprise (future requirement)

**Source:** 2.8.3 — the migration trigger from static to dynamic VLAN assignment.

Static assignment was selected because dynamic assignment requires a RADIUS server
and a directory, neither of which exists in scope (§3). The trigger for revisiting
it is the introduction of that infrastructure, a substantial growth in user count,
or a requirement for individual accountability.

At that point the design moves to a **single** corporate SSID with 802.1X and
RADIUS-assigned VLANs. Sub-stage 2.8.3 notes that the VLAN structure requires no
change — only the assignment mechanism does.

**Why this belongs in a present requirement list.** An access point without
WPA2-Enterprise support would need replacing at the trigger rather than
reconfiguring.

**A second reason, from 2.8.7.** The same change addresses a security weakness the
current design cannot mitigate: evil twin attacks, in which an attacker broadcasts
a duplicate SSID to capture credentials. Defending against this requires
WPA2-Enterprise with server certificate validation, so clients can verify they are
connecting to the legitimate network. Sub-stage 2.8.7 notes that two separate
weaknesses — evil twin resistance and individual accountability — are resolved by
the same migration.

Moving to a single SSID would also eliminate the airtime cost recorded in W1.

### W10 — Configurable native VLAN on the uplink

**Source:** 2.8.5 — *"Native VLAN on all trunks: 999."*

The access point uplink is a trunk, and the native VLAN setting applies to it as
it does to every other trunk in the design (2.3.7). The native VLAN travels
untagged; left at the default of VLAN 1, it enables a double-tagging attack in
which crafted frames reach another VLAN. Setting it to an unused VLAN removes the
destination that attack depends on.

**Consequence for device selection.** The access point must permit the native
VLAN to be changed on its uplink. A device that hard-codes VLAN 1 as native would
leave one trunk in the design without this mitigation while every other trunk has
it — an inconsistency that would be easy to overlook and difficult to notice
later.

### W11 — SSH management, SNMPv3, Syslog, NTP

**Source:** inferred from 2.7.2 rule 6 and the management design in 2.10.

**Access points are not listed in 2.10.3.** That table names routers, switches,
the firewall and servers. The requirement is inferred rather than read.

The inference rests on core ACL rule 6 (2.7.2):

```text
| 6 | Infrastructure devices | 10.0.50.30 | SNMP 162, Syslog UDP 514 | permit |
```

Access points are infrastructure devices: they are managed by IT, they carry
corporate traffic, and their failure affects users. Nothing in the design suggests
they are excluded from the management and monitoring regime — the omission from
the 2.10.3 table appears to be an oversight rather than a decision.

They therefore require the same treatment as every other infrastructure device:

- **SSH** for management, with Telnet and HTTP disabled (2.10.3 principle)
- **SNMPv3** — v1 and v2c rejected because the community string travels in clear
  text (2.10.5)
- **Syslog** to 10.0.50.30 at severity level 6 (2.10.6)
- **NTP client**, synchronising from the edge router (2.10.7)

**Recommended follow-up.** Sub-stage 2.10.3 should be amended to list access
points explicitly, so that a future reader does not have to repeat this inference.
Recorded here rather than changed silently.

## Requirements to Verify Early

| Requirement | Why | If unavailable |
|-------------|-----|----------------|
| **W6** per-SSID client isolation | Frequently global-only; neither fallback is satisfactory | Choose and document which trade-off is accepted |
| **W5** captive portal | Often requires a controller or external server | Guest access functions; the terms-of-use record does not exist |
| **W1** five SSIDs | Technically supported, but above recommended practice | Performance cost, already acknowledged in 2.8.3 |
| **W8** PoE standard | 802.3at may be required rather than 802.3af | Degraded operation rather than clear failure — hard to diagnose |

The handling principle is unchanged: the design is not rewritten to match
available equipment. Where a capability is unavailable, it is recorded in 3.3 as
an implementation limitation with its consequence stated.

## Selection Priority

| Priority | Requirements | Consequence if unmet |
|----------|--------------|----------------------|
| **Essential** | W1, W2, W3 | Wireless users cannot be placed in their department's VLAN; the segmentation established in 2.3 does not extend to wireless |
| **Essential** | W4 | Corporate wireless traffic is unencrypted |
| **Important** | W6, W10 | A documented security control is absent — guest device separation, or VLAN hopping mitigation on one trunk |
| **Important** | W5, W7 | The terms-of-use record or the bandwidth protection specified in 2.8.6 is lost |
| **Important** | W11 | Access points cannot be managed or monitored consistently with the rest of the infrastructure |
| **Physical** | W8 | Access points require local power, constraining placement and compromising coverage |
| **Future** | W9 | Functions today; requires replacement at the migration trigger, and leaves evil twin attacks unaddressed |

W2 is listed as essential alongside W1 because the two are frequently conflated. A
device satisfying W1 but not W2 broadcasts five networks that all lead to the same
place — which is not what the design specifies and would silently collapse the
wireless segmentation.
