# 3.1.5 — Purpose

Why the access point requirement list exists, and what it is used for.

> Companion to `3.1.5-access-points.md`.

---

## What this list does

It defines what each access point must be capable of — eleven requirements, one
list covering both devices.

## Why wireless needs no parallel design

An access point is a **bridge, not a router**. It applies a VLAN tag and hands
frames to the switch, where the existing routing and policy apply exactly as they
do for wired clients.

This is why the design has no wireless-specific security policy. A wireless HR
user lands in VLAN 10 and is subject to the same core ACL rules as a wired HR
user — provided W2 is satisfied.

---

## The two requirements that are easily conflated

**W1** — at least five simultaneous SSIDs.
**W2** — per-SSID VLAN assignment.

A device can satisfy W1 without satisfying W2: broadcasting five network names
that all place traffic into the same VLAN.

If that happened, every wireless user would occupy one segment regardless of
department, and the segmentation established in 2.3 would silently stop extending
to wireless. The wired policy would remain intact and the wireless population would
sit outside it.

Both are listed as essential for this reason.

---

## Where the real constraint lies

**W1 is not a capability problem.** Most access points support eight to sixteen
SSIDs per radio.

The constraint is **airtime**. Each SSID broadcasts beacon frames roughly ten times
per second, at the lowest supported data rate so every device can receive them.
Five SSIDs produce fifty beacons per second before any user data is carried.

Sub-stage 2.8.3 acknowledges this: five exceeds the usual guidance of three to
four, and the cost is accepted knowingly at this user count.

The distinction worth carrying into selection: **what a device can do and what is
sensible to ask of it are different questions.** A model advertising sixteen SSIDs
is not thereby suited to broadcasting sixteen.

---

## The requirement most likely to block

**W6 — client isolation configurable per SSID.**

Many access points implement isolation as a **global** setting. If the selected
model does, neither option is satisfactory:

| Choice | Consequence |
|--------|-------------|
| Enable globally | Corporate wireless users cannot reach each other's devices — peer file sharing and screen sharing break |
| Disable globally | Guest devices can reach one another; the protection in 2.8.6 is absent |

This should be verified before selection rather than discovered during
configuration, because the workaround is a compromise either way.

---

## Where it is used

**At selection (3.3).** W1, W2 and W6 are the discriminating requirements. W8
constrains the switch choice as well, since PoE capability and power budget must
match.

**At implementation.** W10 is easy to miss: the AP uplink is a trunk, so the native
VLAN setting applies to it as it does to every other trunk. Overlooking it would
leave one trunk without the VLAN-hopping mitigation while every other trunk has it.

---

## What W11 revealed

Access points are **not listed** in the management table in 2.10.3. That table
names routers, switches, the firewall and servers.

The requirement was inferred from core ACL rule 6, which permits infrastructure
devices to reach the monitoring server. Access points are infrastructure: managed
by IT, carrying corporate traffic, and affecting users when they fail.

Nothing suggests they were deliberately excluded, so the omission appears to be an
oversight in 2.10.3. The main document records a recommendation to amend it, so a
future reader does not have to repeat the inference.

This is what scanning documents rather than recalling them produces: gaps in the
earlier work become visible.
